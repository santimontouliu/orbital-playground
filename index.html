Read the equations · coordinates, symbols & classification

All four share: r(ν) = p/(1 + e cos ν), with p = h²/μ, ε = v²/2 − μ/r, and e = √(1 + 2εh²/μ²). For ε ≠ 0, a = −μ/(2ε).

Symbols: μ = GM; r is distance from the planet’s center; v is speed; h = |r × v| is specific angular momentum; e is eccentricity; ν is the angle measured from periapsis (the closest point), not the launch angle. A circle has no unique periapsis. Energy is per unit satellite mass: 1 km²/s² = 1 MJ/kg.

Coordinates: polar equations put the planet at the focus. The circular, elliptical, and hyperbolic Cartesian equations use axes centered on the conic and aligned with its principal axes; b is the semi-minor axis (conjugate semi-axis for a hyperbola). In y² = 2pX, the origin is the parabola’s vertex and +X points toward its opening; its focus is at (p/2, 0). These coordinate systems are not the canvas axes.

Study the boundary: use the exact presets, then adjust speed or angle. Equal speed alone does not guarantee a circular orbit: velocity must also be perpendicular to the radius. For a hyperbola, only angles with 1 + e cos ν > 0 describe the physical branch.

Precision: classification uses conserved launch elements, so numerical integration drift cannot turn a parabola into a hyperbola. Circular and parabolic boundaries use a relative tolerance of 10⁻¹⁰. Displayed numbers are rounded; presets keep full precision. Radial motion (h = 0) is marked separately. A mathematical conic can intersect the planet; the actual flight stops on impact.

FIELD NOTES / 01

Orbit is a continuous fall.

Gravity pulls inward while the satellite moves sideways. With enough sideways speed, the surface curves away as fast as the satellite falls.

Try it: position the satellite, aim its arrow, then launch. The blue arrow shows velocity; the amber arrow points toward gravity. Arrow lengths are scaled separately.

Circular speed: √(GM/r). Escape speed: √(2GM/r). Distance r is measured from the planet’s center.

Negative total energy means a bound path, but that path can still intersect the planet. Zero or positive energy permits escape if the satellite avoids collision.

The simulation uses Earth’s radius and mass as reference units. Time advances 300 simulated seconds per real second at 1×. Dashed paths predict motion under the same gravity model.
